# 1. Project Overview

## 1.1 What is Spikesyn?

**Spikesyn is an open-source research-oriented neural signal decoding system
designed to translate recorded brain activity into meaningful digital commands
while investigating the application of neuromorphic computing to brain-computer
interfaces.**

The name _Spikesyn_ combines two concepts fundamental to neuroscience:

- **Spike:** An electrical impulse generated by a neuron, commonly called an
  action potential.
- **Synapse:** A biological connection through which neurons communicate with
  one another.

Spikesyn explores how brain-inspired computational systems can interpret signals
originating from the human nervous system.

The project's initial focus is on non-invasive electroencephalography (EEG), a
technique that measures electrical activity at the scalp.

Using EEG recordings, Spikesyn will attempt to identify patterns associated with
specific motor intentions, such as imagining moving the left or right hand.

These predictions will eventually be translated into digital commands that can
control a simple software interface.

For example:

1. A participant imagines moving their left hand.
2. An EEG system records the resulting patterns of brain activity.
3. Spikesyn processes the recorded signals.
4. A trained decoding algorithm predicts the participant's motor intention.
5. The predicted intention is translated into a digital command, such as `LEFT`.
6. An application receives and executes that command.

The first implementations will use previously recorded EEG datasets rather than
a physical EEG headset.

Spikesyn is not intended to read unrestricted thoughts or reconstruct arbitrary
mental content. Its initial capabilities will be limited to distinguishing
predefined experimental conditions from EEG signals.

## 1.2 Why does Spikesyn exist?

Brain-computer interfaces provide a potential means of interaction between the
human nervous system and external technology.

Such interfaces may eventually contribute to assistive technologies,
communication systems, rehabilitation, and alternative methods of human-computer
interaction.

However, EEG signals present significant challenges:

- Low signal-to-noise ratio.
- Limited spatial resolution.
- Biological and environmental interference.
- Differences in neural activity between individuals.
- Changes in signal characteristics across recording sessions.
- Difficulties extracting meaningful information in real time.

Conventional BCI systems frequently use signal-processing techniques and
machine-learning models to address these challenges.

Spikesyn will investigate an additional computational approach: **spiking neural
networks (SNNs)**.

Unlike conventional artificial neural networks, SNNs explicitly represent
information using discrete spike events and neuron-like dynamical states.

Their computational behavior is inspired by aspects of biological neural
signaling.

The central motivation behind Spikesyn is to investigate whether these
brain-inspired computational models can provide useful alternatives to
conventional approaches to neural signal decoding.

The project does not assume that SNNs are inherently more accurate, efficient,
or biologically realistic in every respect.

Those properties must be investigated experimentally.

# 2. Project Objectives

Spikesyn has two complementary purposes.

## 2.1 Scientific research

Investigate the effectiveness and practical characteristics of neuromorphic
algorithms for EEG-based motor-imagery decoding.

The research should answer questions such as:

- Can conventional machine-learning models reliably distinguish left-hand from
  right-hand motor imagery in the selected EEG dataset?
- Can spiking neural networks learn to distinguish the same conditions?
- How does SNN classification performance compare with conventional methods?
- How do different spike-encoding strategies affect classification performance?
- What computational trade-offs arise from using spiking neural networks?
- How do the models differ in inference latency, parameter count, and
  computational activity?
- How consistently do these methods perform across different participants?

The objective is not necessarily to demonstrate that one approach is superior.

A scientifically valid outcome may show that conventional methods outperform
SNNs, or that the performance difference depends on experimental conditions.

## 2.2 Software engineering

Develop a modular, maintainable, and reproducible Python software system capable
of:

1. Acquiring or loading supported EEG recordings.
2. Validating recording metadata and experimental annotations.
3. Preprocessing multichannel neural signals.
4. Extracting relevant signal segments and features.
5. Training conventional neural decoding algorithms.
6. Training experimental spiking neural networks.
7. Evaluating and comparing different decoding approaches.
8. Generating standardized predictions.
9. Translating predictions into digital commands.
10. Producing reproducible scientific reports and visualizations.

The resulting codebase should demonstrate professional software engineering
practices while remaining accessible to researchers, students, and developers.

# 3. Scientific Research Questions

## 3.1 Primary research question

**Can spiking neural networks decode left-versus-right motor imagery from
non-invasive EEG recordings competitively with conventional machine-learning
classifiers, and what trade-offs arise in accuracy, computational activity,
model complexity, and inference latency?**

## 3.2 Initial hypotheses

### H1 — Conventional EEG decoding

A conventional classification pipeline using Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) and
Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) can extract discriminative information from
left-versus-right motor-imagery EEG recordings.

### H2 — Neuromorphic EEG decoding

A spiking neural network can learn discriminative representations of
motor-imagery EEG signals and achieve performance above an appropriate
chance-level baseline.

### H3 — Computational trade-offs

Spiking neural networks may exhibit different computational characteristics from
conventional classifiers, including differences in inference latency, model
size, and spike-based computational activity.

No assumption should be made that an SNN is automatically more energy-efficient.

Energy-efficiency claims require appropriate hardware measurements or carefully
justified energy models. Spike counts alone are insufficient to establish actual
energy consumption.

# 4. Initial Dataset

## 4.1 PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset

The initial dataset will be the publicly available **EEG Motor Movement/Imagery
Dataset (EEGMMIDB)** hosted by PhysioNet.

Official source:

https://physionet.org/content/eegmmidb/1.0.0/

The dataset includes:

- EEG recordings from 109 participants.
- 64-channel EEG recordings.
- A sampling frequency of 160 Hz.
- Multiple experimental conditions involving actual and imagined movements.
- Event annotations identifying experimental task periods.

The dataset provides a suitable starting point because it is publicly
accessible, well documented, and widely used in BCI research.

## 4.2 Initial experimental task

Spikesyn will initially investigate binary motor-imagery classification:

**Class 1:** Imagined left-fist movement.

**Class 2:** Imagined right-fist movement.

For this task, use motor-imagery runs 4, 8, and 12.

Within those runs, the relevant annotations are:

- `T0`: Rest.
- `T1`: Imagined left-fist movement.
- `T2`: Imagined right-fist movement.

The annotation mapping is task-dependent. It must not be assumed to apply
identically across all runs in the dataset.

The first exploratory recording will be:

**Subject 001 — Run 04**

This recording will be used to understand the dataset and validate the initial
loading and visualization pipeline.

It is not sufficient by itself for a rigorous evaluation of decoding
performance.

## 4.3 Dataset handling requirements

- Download recordings through supported, documented methods.
- Preserve original recording files.
- Do not modify raw EEG data in place.
- Keep downloaded datasets outside Git version control.
- Validate channel names, sampling frequency, recording duration, and event
  annotations.
- Record dataset version and provenance.
- Ensure that preprocessing operations are reproducible.
- Document exclusions and data-quality decisions.

# 5. Scientific Methodology

Spikesyn must follow a research-first development process.

The project must distinguish between exploratory analysis, model development,
and final evaluation.

## 5.1 EEG preprocessing

The initial preprocessing pipeline should investigate:

1. Loading raw EEG recordings.
2. Inspecting recording metadata.
3. Validating channel locations and annotations.
4. Selecting relevant EEG channels.
5. Applying appropriate signal filters.
6. Extracting task-related epochs.
7. Identifying potential artifacts.
8. Preparing standardized inputs for decoding algorithms.

An initial frequency range of approximately **8–30 Hz** is proposed because
motor-imagery research frequently investigates activity in the mu and beta
frequency bands.

This is an initial methodological choice, not a universally optimal filtering
configuration.

Epoch boundaries, filtering choices, and artifact-handling procedures must be
justified and documented.

## 5.2 Conventional decoding baseline

The first decoding model will use:

**Common Spatial Patterns (CSP) + Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA).**

CSP is a spatial filtering technique that identifies channel combinations with
discriminative variance characteristics between two classes.

LDA is a supervised classification algorithm that attempts to separate classes
using linear decision boundaries.

This baseline provides an established reference for evaluating the later
neuromorphic model.

The baseline should be implemented using MNE-Python and scikit-learn.

## 5.3 Evaluation methodology

The initial experiments will use subject-specific evaluation.

For each participant, the three relevant motor-imagery runs will be evaluated
using a leave-one-run-out procedure.

Example:

- Train on runs 4 and 8; evaluate on run 12.
- Train on runs 4 and 12; evaluate on run 8.
- Train on runs 8 and 12; evaluate on run 4.

All trainable preprocessing transformations, feature extraction parameters, and
classifier parameters must be fitted exclusively using training data.

Hyperparameter optimization must not use the held-out evaluation run.

If tuning is necessary, it should occur within an appropriate training-only
validation procedure.

The initial experiment should be validated on one participant before expanding
to a predefined cohort.

## 5.4 Evaluation metrics

At minimum, report:

- Classification accuracy.
- Balanced accuracy.
- Confusion matrices.
- Per-class recall.
- Number of evaluated trials.
- Participant-level results.
- Aggregate results across participants.
- Appropriate uncertainty estimates.

For a balanced binary task, chance-level accuracy is approximately 50%.

However, actual class distributions and statistical uncertainty must be
considered before interpreting results.

A result slightly above 50% does not automatically establish meaningful decoding
performance.

## 5.5 Neuromorphic decoding

After the conventional baseline is validated, Spikesyn will implement an
experimental spiking neural network.

The initial investigation should cover:

- Leaky integrate-and-fire neuron models.
- Membrane potential dynamics.
- Spike generation and reset behavior.
- Spike encoding.
- Temporal processing.
- Surrogate-gradient training.
- Network architecture.
- Model evaluation.

The implementation will use PyTorch and snnTorch.

The first SNN architecture should be intentionally simple.

Complex architectures should only be introduced when justified by experimental
findings.

## 5.6 Model comparison

The conventional and neuromorphic models should be compared under controlled
conditions.

Where possible, both models should use:

- The same participants.
- The same held-out recording runs.
- Consistent class definitions.
- Comparable preprocessing assumptions.
- The same primary evaluation metrics.
- Documented hardware and execution environments.

Any differences in input representation, preprocessing, or training procedure
must be explicitly reported.

The comparison should investigate both predictive performance and computational
characteristics.

# 6. System Architecture

Spikesyn should use a modular Python architecture.

The main components are:

## 6.1 Data acquisition layer

Responsible for:

- Dataset retrieval.
- Local file management.
- Recording discovery.
- Dataset provenance.
- Recording metadata validation.

Initial format: EDF EEG recordings.

## 6.2 Signal processing layer

Responsible for:

- Filtering.
- Channel selection.
- Epoch extraction.
- Signal normalization where appropriate.
- Artifact-handling procedures.
- Preparation of model inputs.

The pipeline must prevent data leakage.

## 6.3 Conventional decoding engine

Responsible for:

- Feature extraction.
- Conventional classifier training.
- Model persistence.
- Prediction generation.
- Baseline evaluation.

Initial algorithms: CSP and LDA.

## 6.4 Neuromorphic decoding engine

Responsible for:

- Spike encoding.
- SNN model definitions.
- Neuron dynamics.
- Model training.
- Model inference.
- Spike-activity measurement.

Initial framework: snnTorch.

## 6.5 Evaluation engine

Responsible for:

- Dataset splitting.
- Experiment execution.
- Performance metrics.
- Model comparisons.
- Result serialization.
- Reproducibility metadata.
- Scientific visualizations.

## 6.6 Command translation layer

Responsible for converting model predictions into standardized digital commands.

Initial command types:

- `LEFT`
- `RIGHT`
- `NO_DECISION`

A command prediction should contain structured information such as:

- Predicted class.
- Timestamp or recording-relative time.
- Decoder identifier.
- Confidence or decision score, when meaningful.
- Relevant experiment metadata.

The `NO_DECISION` state should be used when the system cannot produce a
sufficiently supported prediction.

The decision threshold and confidence calibration must be investigated rather
than assumed.

## 6.7 Demonstration interface

A later milestone will provide a simple demonstration showing how recorded EEG
signals can be converted into computer actions.

Possible demonstrations include:

- Moving a cursor left or right.
- Controlling a simple two-direction application.
- Visualizing predictions during EEG playback.

The initial demonstration will operate on prerecorded data.

It must not be described as a real-time physical BCI unless live acquisition and
processing are actually implemented and validated.

# 10. Research Documentation Standards

Spikesyn should maintain scientific documentation alongside its code.

## 10.1 Literature review

For each relevant scientific source, record:

- Title.
- Authors.
- Publication year.
- DOI or official URL.
- Research objective.
- Dataset.
- Methodology.
- Main findings.
- Limitations.
- Relevance to Spikesyn.

Never invent citations, papers, results, or author information.

## 10.2 Research log

Maintain a chronological research log documenting:

- Questions investigated.
- Experiments conducted.
- Important implementation decisions.
- Unexpected findings.
- Failed experiments.
- Changes in methodology.
- Unresolved questions.
- Next research steps.

## 10.3 Experiment records

Every significant experiment should preserve:

- Experiment identifier.
- Research question.
- Dataset and participant selection.
- Training and evaluation splits.
- Preprocessing configuration.
- Model architecture.
- Hyperparameters.
- Random seeds.
- Software versions.
- Relevant hardware information.
- Evaluation metrics.
- Generated results.
- Interpretation and limitations.

Results should be reproducible from documented commands wherever practical.

## 10.4 Research integrity

Never:

- Fabricate experimental results.
- Invent scientific references.
- Report hypothetical metrics as observed measurements.
- Change evaluation procedures solely to obtain better results without
  disclosure.
- Evaluate a model on data used improperly during training or tuning.
- Claim scientific novelty without investigating prior work.
- Claim real-time functionality from offline experiments.
- Claim actual energy savings without appropriate evidence.

Negative results are legitimate scientific outcomes.

# 13. Initial Scope and Explicit Exclusions

The initial Spikesyn project will not attempt to:

- Develop invasive neural implants.
- Interface directly with implanted electrodes.
- Record brain activity without suitable hardware.
- Read unrestricted human thoughts.
- Diagnose neurological conditions.
- Provide medical advice.
- Build a commercial medical device.
- Claim clinical validation.
- Implement production-grade real-time BCI hardware integration.
- Create a large consumer application.

The initial focus is software-based research using public EEG recordings.

Any future work involving live human participants, medical claims, or
experimental hardware must be assessed separately for safety, privacy, consent,
and applicable research requirements.

# 15. Definition of Project Success

Spikesyn will be considered successful as an initial research and portfolio
project when it provides:

1. **A functional EEG decoding system** capable of generating motor-imagery
   predictions from recorded neural signals.

2. **A validated conventional baseline** using established EEG classification
   methods.

3. **An experimental spiking neural network** evaluated against the conventional
   baseline.

4. **A reproducible research methodology** with documented datasets, splits,
   configurations, and metrics.

5. **A functioning command demonstration** translating neural decoding
   predictions into digital actions.

6. **A maintainable open-source codebase** with meaningful tests and
   documentation.

7. **A scientific-style research report** presenting the methodology,
   experimental findings, interpretation, and limitations.

The project does not need to demonstrate groundbreaking scientific novelty to be
valuable.

Its primary value will come from the combination of scientific rigor,
computational neuroscience, machine learning, and professional software
engineering.
